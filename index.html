The problem

At the Moon's south pole the Sun never rises more than about 1.6° above the ideal horizon. A ridge 300 m high at 10 km distance subtends 1.7° — so terrain, not latitude, decides whether a lander has power. Earth is similarly low and swings ±6.7° with libration, which turns direct-to-Earth communication into a terrain problem too.

Existing tools can compute Sun and Earth positions, but they expose SPICE-level interfaces that make comparison slow. LUNA NAV turns the same physics into a decision surface: pick a site, pick a date, see power and comm windows, and compare eight terrain-backed regions and four reference sites on equal footing.

How the console works

The console opens with a three-step briefing: mission profile, landing site and time window. The profile is not cosmetic — it changes which constraints are scored. A base needs continuous power, thermal stability and direct-to-Earth comms; ice prospecting ignores comms and requires a shadow within 1.38 km; geology trades comms for safe ground and surface time.

After launch, the first-person view uses the real 1,440-bin horizon as a terrain silhouette, an Apollo 11 surface panorama as ground texture, and the Apollo 17 Blue Marble as Earth. The advisor panel screens the selected site against the profile's engineering constraints (EC-01 slope, EC-02 roughness, EC-03 obstacles, EC-04 power, EC-05 DTE, EC-06 PSR access, EC-07 surface stay) and recommends a site and surface-stay window. Every sentence is generated from the payload, not from a language model.

How it works

  1. Terrain. 5 m LOLA DEMs (PGDA, Barker et al. 2021) are ray-marched along 1,440 azimuths to 40 km, with lunar curvature (drop = r²/2R).
  2. Geometry. Sun and Earth vectors come from SPICE DE440 in the MOON_ME frame, with light-time and aberration, at 10-minute resolution.
  3. Visibility. A body counts as visible when its disk clears the terrain, not merely its centre; illumination integrates the visible fraction of the solar disk.
  4. Power. Beam irradiance × incidence angle. A horizontal panel at 2° Sun elevation collects 3.5%; a vertical tracking array collects ~100%.
  5. Decision. Transparent weights turn power, comm and safeness into a comparable score, with a hard 10° slope gate.

Imagery

Surface panorama: Apollo 11, Tranquility Base (NASA/JSC). Earth: Apollo 17 “Blue Marble” (NASA). Polar mosaic: Clementine south-polar mosaic (NASA/JPL/USGS), used as the briefing backdrop; the navigable map is a LOLA 608 km hillshade so site pins stay georeferenced. All are public-domain NASA products.

What this is not

It is not a flight-certified landing analysis. DEM resolution, 80 m far-field terrain, and a 48 × 48 analysis grid bound the accuracy. Numbers are for comparison and education, and every assumption is visible in the Data view.
